Flock Safety, an American technology company and the manufacturer and operator of a line of surveillance cameras and automated license plate recognition systems, has in recent months found itself embroiled in numerous controversies due to its AI-powered products and the various ethical concerns that surround them. Town halls across the United States have erupted with outcry and criticism of the company, with citizens across the United States protesting the use of these cameras in their local communities due to perceived issues with privacy. This hostility to Flock's products has likewise led to numerous cases of camera vandalism and has also been the cause of movements and projects such as "Deflock", with it aiming to track these cameras and map them across the country's. Even the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) has decried Flock's technologies, with the civil rights organization stating upon an initiative meant to combat these cameras that companies like Flock are "quietly trying to build a nationwide mass surveillance system" (Get the flock out 2026). These complaints and protests, however, aren't exactly wrong; Flock Safety's AI-powered products do indeed infringe upon the privacy of the communities where they're installed in and their technologies and the use of Artificial Intelligence within them do indeed have a variety of ethical implications that have led to serious real-world consequences, ones that Flock itself has not adequately addressed or considered. 

The purpose of these Flock cameras, however, aren't necessarily unethical. When considering the five principles that make up the Unified Framework for Ethical AI, as described by Luciano Floridi, Flock's technologies are meant to adhere to the principle of beneficence, with them being designed to promote human well-being, specifically by aiding law enforcement through the use of these cameras. Aside from their specific use-case of tracking license plate information for traffic and automobile-related violations, these cameras and the data they gather can be used by law enforcement to assist in solving a variety of far more serious crimes including thefts, violent crimes, and missing persons incidents. The use of several AI technologies within these cameras can allow them to track and identify specific cars, which in turn can be used by police officers and other law enforcement to assist with their cases. Flock itself has stated that its products have been used to help "authorities locate more than 10,000 missing people last year" (Zahn, 2026) and that "[r]oughly 1 million criminal investigations were supported by Flock in 2025" (Zahn, 2026). 

Because of these results and the role that Flock's technologies have played in assisting Law Enforcement, their usage and deployment may at first glance seem ethical. A closer look, however, does reveal that the company has had challenges with adhering to several other of Floridi's outlined principles, namely that of non-maleficence. Specifically, many across the United States have decried Flock's cameras due to the threat they pose to the privacy of civilians, contradicting this principle of "do no harm". Unlike traditional speeding and traffic enforcement cameras, Flock cameras do not merely target those violating traffic laws but instead they constantly record and thus track every vehicle and person in their line of sight, logging this data into a massive database without their consent or any warrants enabling them to do so. In doing so, Flock's camera systems infringe upon the personal privacy of all citizens of a community, treating every person as a potential suspect in a crime and thus gathering information concerning them, even if there is no evidence of any wrongdoing on their parts. 

Similarly, Flock's treatment of its collected data likewise infringes upon the principle of non-maleficence in how the company has refrained from providing adequate protection to prevent this data from being misused. As noted by the ACLU (Get the flock out 2026), due to the lack of statewide regulation in the US concerning ALPR data protection including topics such as how long this data is saved, how it is saved, and how it is used by, the terms and rules regarding these subjects are instead based on contracts signed by local governments. This has allowed Flock to build a vast network built upon sharing data between police departments across the country, with the ACLU even describing that their "system incentivizes maximum data-sharing" (Get the flock out 2026). To this end, Flock even allows data to be accessed by other agencies across the country, even when a city has opted-out of this feature. 

Moreover, Flock's Camera systems also violate the principle of justice, with how they do not promote fairness and justice and instead have been used for the opposite. Specifically, their technologies enable law-enforcement agencies to target and discriminate against specific groups through the use of the search function due to its lack of limits. One such case of this, as reported by the Electronic Frontier Foundation, is that of the Grand Prairie Police Department in Texas, who used Flock's technology to target Romani peoples, with the non-profit noting that the department "searched for the slur six times while using Flock's "Convoy" feature, which identifies vehicles traveling together—essentially targeting an entire traveling community without specifying any crime" (Hamid, 2025). As such the Electronic Frontier Foundation has concluded that not only do Flock's AI powered camera systems not seek to eliminate discrimination but instead they "makes such discrimination faster and easier to execute at scale" (Hamid, 2025). 

In response to the many criticisms made by citizens and civil liberty organizations nation-wide, Flock's unethical behavior has thus caused them to resort to several risky practices. One such practice is that of Ethics Bluewashing, one that is most notably exemplified in their attempts to address criticism by instituting new policies regarding data protection. As noted by the ACLU, while Flock's promise of reducing it's default retention period from its previous value of one month down to a period of seven days is considered to be a good change, it still does not address many of the underlying ethical concerns regarding their practices and may not even be particularly effective depending on "how its 'Evidence Mode' operates" (Marlow, 2026). Specifically, the ACLU notes that a faulty implementation of their system's new "Evidence Mode" could in fact worsen these data retention issues and that "several of the proposed changes Flock is touting are merely retreads of previous inadequate safety measures" (Marlow, 2026). As such, it is difficult to view these promises as anything else but an attempt at Ethics Bluewashing: using the promise of changes and protection to avoid true accountability. 

The launch and deployment of Flock Safety's camera systems has raised a number of key ethical concerns that has similar led to widespread outcry and protest. The corporation's failure to comply with ethical frameworks such as Floridi's five principles for Ethical AI and their actions such as their use of Ethics Bluewashing have eroded trust and have caused several cities across the United States to end their existing contracts. Any future ventures into the field of AI-powered traffic cameras should learn from Flock's mistakes and should instead build ethical AI systems that protect the privacy of everyday people.
